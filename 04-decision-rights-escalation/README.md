# Decision Rights & Escalation System

### A practical system for improving decision velocity, accountability, and execution.

Organizations slow down when it is unclear who can decide.

Decisions move upward unnecessarily. Leaders become bottlenecks. Teams wait for approval. Issues remain unresolved. Accountability becomes difficult to determine.

The objective of a decision system is simple:

> **Put decisions at the right level, with the right owner, using the right evidence, within the right timeframe.**

The goal is not centralized control.

The goal is **decision clarity and execution speed**.

---

# The Decision System

Every material decision should answer six questions:

1. What decision must be made?
2. Who owns the decision?
3. Who must contribute?
4. What evidence is required?
5. When must the decision be made?
6. Who owns execution afterward?

The operating logic is:

**Issue → Evidence → Recommendation → Decision → Owner → Action → Review**

A decision is incomplete until execution ownership is clear.

---

# 01. Classify the Decision

Not every decision requires the same level of leadership attention.

Classify decisions before deciding who should own them.

| Decision Type | Typical Characteristics | Typical Owner |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic | Changes direction, market, business model, or major priorities | CEO / Board / Executive Team |
| Capital | Material allocation of financial or organizational resources | CEO / CFO / Executive Team |
| Cross-Functional | Requires coordination across multiple functions | Executive Leader |
| Functional | Primarily affects one function or business area | Functional Leader |
| Operational | Recurring execution within established parameters | Team / Manager |
| Exception | Falls outside agreed authority, budget, policy, or risk limits | Escalated Owner |

The objective is not to push every decision upward.

The objective is to place each decision at the **lowest competent level with sufficient authority and context**.

---

# 02. Decision Rights

For every recurring material decision, define four roles.

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Decision Owner | Has authority and accountability for making the decision |
| Contributors | Provide expertise, evidence, or recommendations |
| Execution Owner | Owns implementation after the decision |
| Informed | Must understand the decision but does not participate in making it |

One person should own the final decision.

Multiple contributors may participate.

> **Consult broadly when necessary. Decide clearly.**

Avoid decision structures where everyone participates but nobody owns the outcome.

---

# 03. Decision Rights Matrix

Leadership teams should explicitly define authority for recurring decisions.

Example:

| Decision | Decision Owner | Contributors | Execution Owner | Escalation Trigger |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pricing change | Commercial Leader | Finance, Product | Commercial | Margin below threshold |
| Senior hire | Functional Executive | HR, CEO where required | Functional Executive | Compensation or level outside authority |
| Marketing investment | Marketing Leader | Commercial, Finance | Marketing | Spend exceeds approved budget |
| Customer exception | Commercial Leader | Finance, Operations | Account Owner | Material margin or contractual risk |
| Product priority | Product Leader | Commercial, Technology | Product | Material strategic or resource conflict |
| New market entry | CEO / Executive Team | Finance, Commercial, Operations | Market Owner | Strategic commitment or material capital required |

The exact matrix should reflect the organization.

The principle should not change:

**Authority must be explicit before the decision is required.**

---

# 04. Delegation Levels

Delegation should define how much authority exists, not simply whether authority exists.

A practical five-level model:

| Level | Authority |
|---|---|
| 1. Recommend | Analyze the issue and recommend a decision |
| 2. Decide With Approval | Make the recommendation, obtain approval before acting |
| 3. Decide and Inform | Make the decision within agreed parameters, then inform leadership |
| 4. Decide and Execute | Own the decision and implementation |
| 5. Full Ownership | Own the outcome, decision system, resources, and continuous improvement |

Leadership should deliberately move capable leaders toward higher levels of ownership.

If every material decision requires CEO approval, the organization has not scaled decision authority.

---

# 05. Decision Guardrails

Delegation works when boundaries are clear.

For each material area, define guardrails such as:

- Budget authority
- Pricing authority
- Hiring authority
- Contract authority
- Customer concessions
- Investment thresholds
- Risk limits
- Policy exceptions
- Strategic boundaries
- Regulatory or legal requirements

Example:

| Area | Authority | Guardrail | Escalate When |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discounting | Commercial Leader | Up to agreed margin threshold | Margin falls below threshold |
| Hiring | Functional Leader | Within approved headcount and compensation band | New headcount or compensation exception |
| Marketing | Marketing Leader | Within approved monthly budget | Incremental investment required |
| Customer Terms | Commercial Leader | Standard commercial terms | Legal, cash, or material risk changes |
| Operating Spend | Functional Leader | Within approved budget | Budget increase required |

Good delegation defines freedom **inside clear boundaries**.

---

# 06. Escalation System

Escalation should occur because a defined threshold has been crossed.

It should not occur simply because a decision is difficult.

Escalate when:

- Authority limits are exceeded
- Material financial exposure changes
- Strategic priorities conflict
- Multiple functions cannot resolve an issue
- Customer or reputational risk becomes material
- Legal or regulatory exposure exists
- A critical commitment will be missed
- Required resources exceed approved levels
- The decision is irreversible or unusually difficult to reverse
- The issue threatens a material business outcome

Do not escalate routine execution.

Do not escalate responsibility.

> **Escalate the constraint, not the ownership.**

The person closest to the issue should normally remain responsible for driving resolution unless ownership itself needs to change.

---

# 07. Escalation Thresholds

Leadership should define materiality before problems occur.

Example:

| Area | Normal Authority | Escalation Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | Commercial Team | Material forecast deterioration |
| Margin | Business Owner | Margin below agreed threshold |
| Cash | CFO / Business Owner | Material liquidity or working-capital risk |
| Customer | Account / Commercial Owner | Material revenue, contractual, or reputational exposure |
| People | Functional Leader | Senior leadership, compensation, conduct, or critical capability issue |
| Delivery | Operations | Critical customer commitment at risk |
| Strategy | Executive Team | Material deviation from agreed strategic direction |
| Investment | Budget Owner | Spend exceeds approved authority |

Thresholds should be measurable whenever possible.

Ambiguous escalation rules create unnecessary executive dependency.

# 08. Decision Velocity

Some decisions become expensive because they remain open too long.

Every material decision should have a decision date.

Track:

| Decision | Owner | Required By | Status | Cost of Delay | Next Step |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | Open / Decided / Blocked | | |
| | | | | | |
| | | | | | |

Ask:

**What happens if we do not decide this week?**

The cost of delay may include:

- Lost revenue
- Margin erosion
- Customer risk
- Idle resources
- Delayed hiring
- Slower execution
- Opportunity cost
- Leadership time
- Organizational uncertainty

Decision speed matters when delay changes the economics or execution of the business.

---

# 09. Decision Quality

Speed without discipline creates poor decisions.

For material decisions, require a minimum decision standard.

### Decision

What exactly must be decided?

### Context

Why does the decision matter now?

### Evidence

What facts materially affect the decision?

### Options

What realistic alternatives exist?

### Recommendation

What does the owner recommend?

### Trade-Offs

What are we accepting or giving up?

### Risks

What could materially go wrong?

### Decision

What was decided?

### Execution Owner

Who owns implementation?

### Review Date

When will we evaluate whether the decision worked?

This should be concise.

The purpose is to improve thinking, not create paperwork.

---

# 10. One-Way and Two-Way Decisions

Decision process should reflect reversibility.

### Two-Way Decisions

Easy or relatively inexpensive to reverse.

Examples may include:

- Small operating experiments
- Channel tests
- Process changes
- Limited pricing tests
- Tactical resource adjustments

These decisions should usually move quickly.

### One-Way Decisions

Difficult, expensive, or disruptive to reverse.

Examples may include:

- Acquisitions
- Major market entry
- Significant capital commitments
- Senior executive appointments
- Material organizational restructuring
- Long-term strategic partnerships

These decisions require greater evidence and scrutiny.

Do not apply one-way-door governance to reversible operating decisions.

---

# 11. Executive Bottleneck Diagnostic

Leadership should periodically test whether senior executives are slowing the organization.

Ask:

1. Which decisions repeatedly reach the CEO?
2. Which of those genuinely require CEO authority?
3. Where are teams waiting for approval?
4. Which decisions lack a clear owner?
5. Where do multiple executives believe they own the same decision?
6. Which decisions are repeatedly reopened?
7. Where are authority limits unclear?
8. Which escalations could have been resolved lower in the organization?
9. Where does missing information delay decisions?
10. Which leaders need greater authority or capability?

A CEO should not be the organization's default routing system.

Executive attention should concentrate on decisions where executive judgment materially changes the outcome.

The decision log prevents three common problems:

- Decisions being forgotten
- Decisions being reopened without new evidence
- Nobody knowing whether the decision worked

The objective is organizational memory and accountability.

---

# 13. Decision Review

Do not review every decision.

Review decisions when:

- The outcome is strategically important
- Significant uncertainty existed
- The decision produced an unexpected result
- The organization can learn something material
- Similar decisions will recur

Review four questions:

1. What did we expect?
2. What happened?
3. Why was it different?
4. What should change in future decisions?

The objective is not to assign blame.

The objective is to improve organizational judgment.

## Close

Before leaving the review:

**Are all required decisions explicit?**

**Does every decision have one owner?**

**Is the required evidence sufficient?**

**Are escalation reasons clear?**

**Does execution have an owner?**

**Are deadlines explicit?**

**Do material decisions have review dates?**

If these questions cannot be answered, the decision process is incomplete.

---

# Executive Standard

At any point, leadership should be able to answer:

**What decisions are currently material?**

**Who owns each decision?**

**Which decisions are blocked?**

**What requires escalation?**

**Why does it require executive attention?**

**What decisions are overdue?**

**Who owns execution after each decision?**

**When will we know whether the decision worked?**

If leadership cannot answer these questions consistently, decision rights are unclear.

---

# Operating Principle

> **Push decisions to the lowest competent level.**
>
> **Define the boundaries.**
>
> **Escalate material constraints.**
>
> **Keep ownership clear.**
>
> **Review outcomes and improve judgment.**
